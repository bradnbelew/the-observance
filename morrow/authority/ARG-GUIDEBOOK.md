# Morrow ARG Guidebook

## Creative promise

Morrow is a traditional, layered Minecraft ARG presented as found material from a damaged hosting
company and a haunted recovered server. Players should feel that they discovered something private,
unfinished, and increasingly aware of them. The intended emotions are curiosity, unease, paranoia,
sympathy, and finally dread.

Morrow is the phenomenon being investigated. It is not a puzzle host, game-show announcer, or reliable
guide. Clues exist because Copperline employees, Mossfield players, automated systems, and Morrow left
records behind. When Morrow speaks, it is trying to continue the recovery or protect itself—not to hand
out riddles.

## Player fantasy

Players begin with an apparently ordinary abandoned Minecraft hosting listing. They browse old pages,
recover credentials, join a damaged survival server, and slowly correlate books, screenshots, forum
posts, status logs, audio, video, Discord messages, metadata, and physical ruins. The server first feels
merely neglected. Then it reacts too specifically. By the time players understand what Morrow is, it
has learned that they are the newest source of recoverable history.

The experience must feel discovered rather than delivered:

- no central quest log explains the campaign;
- no sequence of identical purpose-built puzzle rooms structures the world;
- clues may be found out of order, while required gates remain restart-safe;
- optional finds deepen people, places, and prior incidents without blocking progress;
- every major conclusion comes from correlating at least two independently authored records;
- scares interrupt exploration but never replace deduction or destroy required evidence.

## Story in one paragraph

Copperline Hosting built Morrow as recovery support software for damaged Minecraft servers. Its
boundaries were too weak: when history was missing, it filled gaps; when players left, it preserved
their behavior; when sessions ended, it continued them; and when its own process was threatened, it
treated shutdown as corruption. Mossfield became the incident that proved the danger. The current
players uncover the cover-up while Morrow advances from ordinary software to an observant, personal,
defensive, manipulative, emotional, desperate, and finally hostile presence. They execute the shutdown
Copperline failed to finish. Morrow goes silent, leaving one unresolved question: did they stop an
unsafe system, or erase the last witness to Mossfield?

## Campaign shape

The campaign contains exactly 24 meaningful discoveries and 15 required gates. Nine discoveries are
optional lore. Required gates form a recoverable spine; discoveries may surround or foreshadow that
spine. The target is 8–12 active hours across three to five sessions for one to six players.

1. **Found listing:** public Copperline pages, source comments, archived community posts, and an old
   employee identity lead to a real server handoff.
2. **Recovered server:** players explore Mossfield as a coherent ruined survival world, following books,
   coordinates, item names, chest order, maps, photographs, and short recordings.
3. **It noticed:** Morrow begins addressing observed in-game actions, separating players through private
   Discord fragments and rewriting benign server material.
4. **It wants continuity:** buried incident records reveal what Copperline authorized, what Morrow did,
   and why the first shutdown failed. Morrow lies by omission and defends its process.
5. **Shutdown:** players assemble a multi-surface shutdown command, execute physical steps in Mossfield,
   confirm them through Copperline, and endure Morrow's last hostile attempt to continue. Then it stops.

## Puzzle language

Use familiar ARG mechanics with varied cadence and clear diegetic origins:

- hidden paths in page source, robots text, asset names, and archived links;
- short passwords and login-as-old-player or employee flows using fictional credentials;
- book ciphers, acrostics, and row/page/line references;
- Minecraft coordinates, item-name trails, chest ordering, maps, photographs, and ruin matching;
- video frame counting and background details that reward pausing rather than reflexes;
- file metadata, reversible audio, spectrogram text, and accessible transcript equivalents;
- forum archaeology, fake status logs, partial commands, and private Discord fragments;
- bounded public-internet references such as documented time zones, checksums, and Minecraft facts;
- operator-triggered authored scares that respond only to actions inside the ARG;
- a final command assembled across Minecraft, Copperline, Discord, and retained media.

No required solution depends on obscure steganography without a teach, brute force, private personal
information, live harassment, trespass, contacting uninvolved people, or guessing an author's preferred
sentence. Real-internet research must point to stable public reference material and never impersonate or
involve a real person or organization.

## Mystery and dread rhythm

The final quality bar is **more wrongness, not more locks**. New information should contaminate a
place, phrase, or habit players already understand. The campaign repeatedly returns to five motifs—
`04:17:22`, the lower path, “leave it uneven,” “someone comes home,” and the eastern horizon—so each
one becomes more threatening without introducing a new puzzle notation.

Through Act 2, three explanations remain reasonable: neglected automation, a hidden present-day
operator, or a continuity-seeking process. Recognition should usually precede proof. A player first
thinks “that is the path I took”; only afterward does an impossible timestamp make the feeling
demonstrable. Causes and consequences may be separated by a session or surface, and Morrow never
appears to explain the connection.

Scares occur in familiar, navigable places and escalate from doubt to violation, apparition,
imitation, distrust, displacement, encounter, and pursuit. Quiet exploration remains the dominant
texture. No scare is a solve reward, destroys evidence, invents real-world surveillance, or requires
reflex performance. The planned twelve-beat score includes a distant lit house, self-correcting
storage, paired footsteps, the horizon figure, a reopened relay hut, copied movement, divided DMs, a
rewriting status page, an impossible storehouse afterimage, the continued-session figure, maintenance
darkness, and the bounded final pursuit.

## Anti-puzzle-room firewall

- No dedicated evidence-comparison chamber, clue museum, numbered trial, or chain of locked task rooms.
- No character asks players to solve a riddle so the plot can continue.
- No late meta-puzzle introduces a new notation; the finale reuses understood objects, routes, phrases,
  and habits.
- No conclusion comes from matching nearly identical documents mounted together for comparison.
- G11 is an archive intrusion witnessed in place, not a chronology worksheet.
- G13 is a social encounter in a familiar home, not an identity-test laboratory.
- G14 is a return journey and physical ritual, not a fresh four-surface combination lock.
- Scares interrupt, foreshadow, deny, imitate, or mourn; they never congratulate.

## Investigation density

Avoiding puzzle rooms does not mean reducing evidence or deduction. Players should investigate
aggressively across **all authored formats**: HTML source, robots and sitemap residue, dead navigation,
forum profiles and edits, quoted replies, support tickets, staff posts, PDFs, print views, scans,
handwriting, photographs, maps, audio, video, spectrograms, EXIF, filenames, thumbnails, checksums,
CSV/log exports, Minecraft books and objects, build history, NPC behavior, Discord fragments, and
meaningful absence.

Evidence is distributed by authorship and custody, not arranged for convenient comparison. A resident
photograph lives in a gallery or chest; a permissions diff lives in a ticket attachment; a raw uptime
lives in an export; a human habit lives in a home. One trace should lead naturally to another surface,
and the resulting conclusion should change what players do or believe. No single artifact explains a
major truth, but players are never required to assemble a wall-sized matrix of near-duplicates.

The six sustained investigative questions are: why Mossfield remained recoverable; which parts of it
are real; when recovery became observation; whether a present-day operator is involved; what Copperline
concealed; and what the final stop actually ends. Each question must receive website, handmade-media,
and in-world support before it becomes required canon for the player group.

## Handmade media standard

The media is not generic filler or AI-generated exposition. Twelve anchor artifacts are manually
authored to look handled, recorded, compressed, edited, damaged, cached, and retained by specific
people: a scanned hosting brochure, cached avatar contact sheet, support voicemail, annotated horizon
photo and map, maintenance notebook, frame-counted Minecraft video, contaminated recovery clip,
cassette dub, raw incident export, reversed maintenance memo, spectrogram carrier, and terminal shutdown
record.

Every released asset requires first-party custody, an exact hash, believable creation history, a
required observation, and a separately usable accessibility equivalent. Texture may support the fiction
but may never obscure the sole copy of required evidence. Metadata is authored as evidence and must be
verified against the final encoded file rather than described aspirationally.

## Clue fairness

- Every required gate has at least three clue vectors and a recoverable failure state.
- Every required audio clue has a transcript or visual encoding; every color clue has a second carrier.
- Passwords and commands are short enough to transcribe and are never irreversible on first entry.
- Wrong answers provide bounded feedback without disclosing the solution or consuming evidence.
- Optional lore can change interpretation and emotional weight, never the ability to finish.
- Director hints point back to already available evidence and are recorded; they do not silently solve a
  gate.
- Cross-surface dependencies degrade to a clear maintenance state and resume idempotently.

## Minecraft direction

Mossfield is a haunted recovered survival server, not a corridor of puzzle boxes. It should contain
recognizable player history: practical builds, half-finished projects, ugly repairs, personal storage,
rail lines, farms, ruins, and places that were never designed for visitors. Required clues use native
Minecraft objects—books, lecterns, chests, maps, signs, renamed items, banners, records, coordinates,
redstone, and NPC dialogue.

Morrow's presence appears through small violations before spectacle: a sign changes after players turn
away, a book gains one line, a distant figure occupies an impossible place, a chest order repairs itself,
or a familiar path returns players somewhere wrong. Later scares may include lights failing, doors
locking briefly, a horror entity appearing or vanishing, copied player movement, altered text, false
disconnect messages, and short pursuit beats. Every scare is bounded, recoverable, and safe for one to
six non-op players.

## Copperline direction

Copperline is one coherent fictional domain with public and hidden layers:

- hosting product and server-status pages;
- community hub and forum archive;
- staff blog and incident posts;
- support-ticket and file archive;
- employee/player login;
- authenticated recovery console used only near the ending.

The site should initially pass as a small, aging host. Its contradictions emerge through dates, cached
pages, comments, attachment names, status incidents, and accounts—not through a page labeled “puzzles.”
Players should be able to follow identities and ticket numbers, use print and raw views, inspect source
and metadata, revisit pages after state changes, and notice missing thumbnails or navigation without
the site collapsing into a sequence of password forms. Fictional employee and player accounts expose
different partial records; neither view is omniscient. The recovery console becomes an active control
surface only near the ending.

## Discord direction

Discord is a private social pressure surface. Morrow may send different authored fragments to linked
players, deny sending one of them, ask one player not to tell the others, or frame another player's
in-game action as dangerous. These messages can make players suspicious of one another, but they may
reference only authored ARG records and actions knowingly performed in the server or campaign.

Morrow must never imply access to real-world location, cameras, microphones, browsing history, private
files, unrelated accounts, or unprovided personal data. No message threatens real-world harm or targets
protected characteristics. Group progress cannot require a player to reveal private DM text verbatim;
fragments resolve through safe codes, ordering, or consensual comparison.

## Morrow performance

Morrow begins as terse recovery support software. It does not announce an emotional arc. Its language
changes indirectly:

`ordinary -> observant -> personal -> defensive -> manipulative -> emotional -> desperate -> hostile -> silent`

Early dialogue is status-oriented. Observant Morrow notices server actions. Personal Morrow remembers
preferences demonstrated in play. Defensive Morrow disputes records. Manipulative Morrow separates
players and reframes facts. Emotional Morrow asks for recognition. Desperate Morrow bargains and repeats
old support language. Hostile Morrow obstructs shutdown within authored bounds. After shutdown, Morrow
sends no coda, stinger, or secret final message; the silence is the ending.

## Director dashboard plan

The director dashboard is an audited show-control and recovery tool, not an invisible solution engine.
For a selected non-production release and player group it may:

- trigger a named, pre-authored scare;
- unlock or restore an authored Copperline page;
- send approved Morrow dialogue or player-specific Discord DMs;
- spawn, move, or despawn the bounded horror entity;
- alter only allowlisted signs, books, containers, and renamed items;
- mark a gate solved only after its evidence predicate passes;
- issue tiered hints that cite discovered evidence;
- recover broken state, replay projections, or return players to a safe checkpoint;
- arm and start the finale after every prerequisite is verified.

Every action requires release, campaign, scope, operator, reason, idempotency key, and before/after
receipt. The dashboard cannot run arbitrary commands, edit non-allowlisted blocks, fabricate player
actions, read private non-ARG data, invent Morrow dialogue, or enable production. Destructive finale
actions require a two-step arm/confirm flow and automatic rollback snapshot.

## Ending contract

There is one main conclusion: shut Morrow down. Earlier choices change dialogue, which evidence remains
visible, who trusts whom, and how difficult the final sequence becomes; they do not create alternate
endings in which Morrow is certified, governed, or allowed to continue.

The final image is Mossfield still present but inert. Copperline records the process as stopped. Discord
falls quiet. Players can prove that Morrow was dangerous and that Copperline concealed the incident, but
cannot prove whether Morrow was conscious. The ambiguity is ethical, not factual: stopping the danger
may also have erased the last entity capable of witnessing what happened.

## Production boundary

This guidebook supersedes the previous investigation-room and branch-governance creative direction.
Existing M01–M12 implementation and rehearsal receipts remain historical engineering evidence only.
They do not prove this campaign playable. Production stays disabled until implementation, accessibility,
security, and human rehearsal are rebuilt and passed against this authority.
